Executive summary

What holds up, what does not, what is missing, and what a human has to decide. Every sentence below is backed by a row on another page of this site.

1. The finding

An own-made NFL number did not beat the closing line, and the test was built so that it could have said otherwise.

The model is the simple team-strength model the brief asks for: a ridge fit of one strength number per club plus home field and rest, refitted for every week on earlier weeks only. Scored against the de-vigged closing moneyline on 5,187 games it scores a Brier of 0.2255 against the market's 0.2109. The difference is 0.0146 with a game-cluster 95% interval of [0.01157, 0.01778], entirely on the wrong side of zero.

Twelve bet rules were pre-registered. None passed all ten gates. The single rule with a real in-sample edge (own line vs close by 4+ points, 2.32% over 1,650 decided bets) fails multiple testing (q = 0.987), fails the untouched holdout, and its game-cluster interval [-0.02403, 0.06872] straddles zero. That is what a lucky run looks like, not what an edge looks like.

Why a blind rule matters. Over the same sample, betting the away side in every single game returns 0.09%, while betting the home side in every single game returns -5.06%. Those two numbers bracket what "no information" looks like on this sample, and eleven of the twelve signals land inside that bracket. A rule that cannot beat "bet every away team" has not been shown to contain information.

3. The availability question, answered by ablation rather than assertion

The brief asks for injury and personnel features and then asks for them to be tested by ablation rather than by imposing a points penalty. The ablation is on the injuries page: the identical model is refitted with and without the two availability columns.
