Executive summary

A full-sample test of whether schedule strength can beat the NFL closing line. The architecture is sound, the evidence is negative, and the negative is worth publishing.

What was asked

The brief asks for four things: find out what is missing before a decision can be made; originate independent NFL lines; run strategies through validation gates with correlation and luck removed; and track injuries and depth-chart deficiencies. This study answers each with a number and a link.

What was found

  • Own lines are not better than the market. Over 7,103 games the closing line beats the model in every season: holdout spread MAE 9.63 (market) versus 10.714 (model).
  • No schedule signal survives the gates. 0 of 11 rules pass; the best rule's 95% ROI interval still crosses zero.
  • Schedule strength is not predictive of results. The correlation between rating difference and actual margin is r=0.466 against the spread, and the model's edge over the market is negative.
  • Three published defects were found and are flagged. Stale standings (10 fields), a stale provenance pin, and three injury rows that changed on the official page.
  • Two whole tracks cannot be built from the current sources. Prop combos need prices; depth-chart deficiency needs game-day inactives and dated depth charts.

What it means

The absence of a schedule edge is not a failure of the model — it is the expected result in a market that prices information efficiently. The value of the exercise is the gate set: it rejected a 94%-hit-rate result in this very session because the hit rate was arithmetically impossible. Any future rule set should be registered against the same eight gates before it is evaluated.
